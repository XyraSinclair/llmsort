Sorting with Jev: which recipe buys the most information per dollar?

Eleven sorting recipes on hosted Jev (TypeSafe jev-latest, $0.042 per million input tokens), run round by round on 198 countries by population (a true cardinal reference) and 150 arXiv abstracts by novelty (a generating judge as reference), each checkpoint priced; plus the same 324 windows re-asked two days apart. Measured 2026-09-21. $2.05 in total. Written for anyone who ranks things with model judgments and pays per token.

How good is Jev at pairwise ratio sorting: as good as the reference it is checked against, transitive, cheap, and cardinal only when anchored. Every recipe reaches the same ceiling — ρ .97–.98 against true population, .88–.91 against gemma-4-31b on abstracts (gemma’s own reliability), self-agreement .99–1.00 and .99–1.00 — so the ratio ladder is not more accurate than a yes/no or a rating; it is one of several ways to read the same latent. Its fitted log-ratios are compressive: .40 nat per nat of true log population on a 1/8…8 ladder, .76 on a 1/100…100 ladder against three pinned anchors.

The recipe that buys the most information per dollar is the plainest one: k items in a window, one ten-level rating each, random windows, repeat. ρ ≥ .97 on countries costs $0.0024 at k = 24 and $0.0041 at k = 8; self-agreement ≥ .95 on 150 abstracts costs $0.0121. The all-pairs ratio ladder reaches the same numbers at 5× the price; yes/no on all pairs at 1.2×; a ratio cycle (2k questions) at 3×. Adaptive windows (sort, then compare neighbours) bought nothing here because the ceiling is reached in two or three random rounds.

Pinned anchors with a wide ladder are the recipe for a cardinal answer, and the most self-consistent one (self .998 on abstracts, .999 on countries); a round costs 3.3× a rating round, but it clears the accuracy threshold in its first, so the cost to threshold ($0.0059) is within 1.5× of the rating’s. Time drift is nil: the same request two days later returns the same probabilities (r .995–.998 over 39,000 answers), and the sorts fitted from each day agree at .991+, so a cached answer is a permanent measurement.

Setup

Cohorts. countries: 198 sovereign states from Wikidata with their latest population; the reference is log population, so Spearman measures accuracy against a fact and the fitted slope measures cardinal calibration. arxiv150: 150 arXiv abstracts scored on a one-paragraph definition of novelty; the reference is gemma-4-31b’s pooled z-score on the 40 that overlap the multi-criteria benchmark, so ρ is judge–judge and self-agreement is the precision statistic for all 150.

Recipes. A round shows every item once, in windows of k. rate: one ten-level standing question per item (“where does item x stand among those shown”), k questions a call. yes/no all pairs: “x is stronger than y” for every ordered pair, k(k−1). ratio all pairs: the polarity-safe nine-rung ladder (“far weaker: 1/8 or less” … “far stronger: 8 times or more”) for every ordered pair. ratio cycle: the same ladder on a random Hamiltonian cycle only, both orders, 2k. adaptive variants re-fit after each round and window consecutive blocks of the current order with a random offset. anchored wide ratio: three items pinned at the 10 / 50 / 90 % points of a one-round rating pilot (charged) sit in every window beside k−3 targets, each target against each anchor and the anchors against each other on a 1/100 … 100 ladder.

Fit and score. Pairs are least squares on the directed log-ratio (ladder expectation) or logit (yes/no); ratings are least squares with a window fixed effect, which is what makes sorted windows usable. After every round: Spearman and Pearson against the reference, split-half by round parity stepped up by Spearman–Brown (self), bits per item as ½ log₂ 1/(1−r²), and the dollars billed so far. The first round of adaptive recipes is random by construction.

1 · Agreement against dollars

On countries every recipe climbs to the same ceiling and the x position of the climb is the whole result: rating at k = 24 is at ρ .97 after 2 rounds and $0.0024, rating at k = 8 after 3 rounds, the all-pairs ratio after 2 rounds but $0.0193, because a round of 56 ladder questions on 8 items costs 7× a round of 8 ratings and carries no more about the order. Yes/no on all pairs is the cheap all-pairs form (a yes/no question is a third of a ladder question in tokens) and is the second-cheapest recipe overall. On abstracts the same ordering holds against a softer ceiling: everything sits at ρ .88–.91 against gemma from round 3 on, and the self-agreement panel is where the recipes separate — rating reaches .95 first, the anchored ladder reaches the highest final value.

So: the information per dollar is set by the number of questions a call needs to place its items, not by their form. A rating places k items with k questions; every pairwise design spends at least 2k and gets the same latent.

2 · Dollars to a threshold

Read as bits: at its first round the k = 24 rating carries 177k bits of population information per dollar (198 items × 1.1 bits per item for $0.0012), k = 8 rating 169k, yes/no all pairs 101k, the ratio cycle 69k, the all-pairs ratio 26k. The metric saturates by design — bits per item is bounded by what the judge knows, so past the ceiling every added dollar buys zero — which is why the threshold cost is the operational number.

3 · Cardinal calibration

The 1/8 … 8 ladder compresses to .40–.40 nat per nat whether asked on all pairs or a cycle, adaptive or not: a country ten times as populous is called “about 2 times” on average. The wide ladder against pinned anchors recovers .76 at k = 8 and .71 at k = 24, and it is the only recipe whose ratio answers can be read as magnitudes without a calibration step. Yes/no logits are on their own scale (.85 logit per nat) and are ordinal in practice.

So: for an ordinal answer any recipe is calibrated enough; for a cardinal one, pin anchors, widen the ladder to the range the truth spans, and still expect a quarter of the dynamic range to be missing.

4 · Adaptive windows

Sorting after each round and comparing neighbours is the classic way to spend comparisons where the order is uncertain. Here it bought little: adaptive rating reaches the threshold at the same round and price as random windows on both cohorts, and adaptive ratio cycles clear the countries threshold one round earlier ($0.0091 against $0.0121), still 2× the rating. Two reasons are visible in the data. The ceiling arrives in two or three random rounds, before an adaptive design has anything to exploit. And at k = 24 the adaptive rating’s self-agreement falls to .945 on countries and .972 on abstracts against .997 and .997 for random windows: windows of near-equal items give a rating question nothing to spread its levels over, and the window fixed effect then absorbs most of what it does say.

So: random windows, repeated, are the design; adaptive refinement is worth revisiting only for a cohort where the plateau takes more than five rounds.

5 · Time drift

Two days apart, a yes/no probability moves on average 0.012 and a nine-level expectation 0.09 of a level; the fitted sorts agree at .991 or better and agree with Fable to the same third decimal. That is bf16 batching wobble (the .03 already known), not drift. A cached answer can be reused indefinitely; there is no reason to re-ask a question for freshness.

6 · Can Jev tell when to hand off?

A system that uses Jev where it can and a stronger model where it cannot needs a signal for “cannot”. Jev does not carry one per item: the rank correlation between its own uncertainty and its error against Fable is -0.04–.24, and escalating by it is the random curve. The information exists — an oracle handing over six of 24 items closes most of the gap to Fable — but Jev’s errors are systematic opinions it holds with the same confidence as its correct ones, which is the same fact as “more consistent than right” seen from the other side. Disagreement with a second cheap judge (gemma) is a slightly better signal (.14–.23) and beats random by a few hundredths on the prose cohorts. At the level of a whole attribute the picture is better: Jev–gemma agreement predicts Jev–Fable agreement at r 0.51 over the 36 cells.

So: decide Jev-or-frontier per attribute, from a small pilot against the strong judge, not per item from Jev’s confidence; a second cheap judge is worth more as a disagreement detector than as a second vote.

Recipes llmsort should endorse

  1. Ordinal sort, any size: random windows of 8–24 items, one ten-level standing question per item, window fixed effect in the fit, two or three rounds. About $0.0024–$0.0041 for 200 items with a fact behind them, $0.0121 to self-agreement .95 on 150 abstracts.
  2. When a per-pair read is wanted (a specific comparison must be defensible, or the criterion may be unstated): yes/no on all ordered pairs in the window, logit fit. Second-cheapest recipe; both orders repair the yes-lean.
  3. Cardinal answer: three pinned anchors from a one-round rating pilot, wide ladder spanning the true range, every target against every anchor. Highest self-agreement, slope .76 against truth.
  4. Escalation: pilot each new attribute on ~24 items against a frontier judge; keep Jev where it agrees, hand the attribute (not individual items) to the stronger model where it does not.
  5. Do not spend on: all-pairs ratio ladders for an ordinal answer (5× the price for the same rank information); adaptive windows before the random plateau is measured; re-asking for freshness.
