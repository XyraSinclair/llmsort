Sorting with Jev: which recipe buys the most information per dollar?

Eleven sorting recipes on hosted Jev (TypeSafe jev-latest, $0.042 per million input tokens), run round by round on 198 countries by population (a true cardinal reference) and 150 arXiv abstracts by novelty (a generating judge as reference), each checkpoint priced; the same 324 windows re-asked two days apart; one attribute asked eight ways on countries and on 169 first names by four perceived qualities against a frontier judge; and a closing grid of 29 judgment designs — pairwise, triple, setwise, choice, yes/no, ladders, level counts, state sizes, formats — on four cohorts at equal spend. Measured 2026-09-21 and 22. $2.55 in total. Written for anyone who ranks things with model judgments and pays per token.

The best sorting system on Jev, across every way of asking — one item rated among many, two at a time, three at a time, choose-the-highest, yes/no, ratio ladders, 3 to 10 levels, states of 2 to 48 items — is a 24-item labelled state with one ten-level standing question per item, random windows, repeated. On the § 10 grid (29 recipes × four cohorts × every budget) no other recipe beats it by more than .027 anywhere — inside the judge’s own .03 wobble — and every other family loses to it by .05–.35 somewhere, most of all on the soft attribute, where pairwise, anchored and choice designs sit .1–.35 below it. It costs $0.0012 a round for 200 items and reaches its ceiling in two.

How good is Jev at pairwise ratio sorting: as good as the reference it is checked against, transitive, cheap, and cardinal only when anchored. Every recipe reaches the same ceiling — ρ .97–.98 against true population, .88–.91 against gemma-4-31b on abstracts (gemma’s own reliability), self-agreement .99–1.00 and .99–1.00 — so the ratio ladder is not more accurate than a yes/no or a rating; it is one of several ways to read the same latent. Its fitted log-ratios are compressive: .40 nat per nat of true log population on a 1/8…8 ladder, .76 on a 1/100…100 ladder against three pinned anchors.

The recipe that buys the most information per dollar is the plainest one: k items in a window, one ten-level rating each, random windows, repeat. ρ ≥ .97 on countries costs $0.0024 at k = 24 and $0.0041 at k = 8; self-agreement ≥ .95 on 150 abstracts costs $0.0058. The all-pairs ratio ladder reaches the same numbers at 5× the price; yes/no on all pairs at 1.2×; a ratio cycle (2k questions) at 3×. Adaptive windows (sort, then compare neighbours) bought nothing here because the ceiling is reached in two or three random rounds.

Pinned anchors with a wide ladder are the recipe for a cardinal answer, and the most self-consistent one (self .998 on abstracts, .999 on countries); a round costs 3.3× a rating round, but it clears the accuracy threshold in its first, so the cost to threshold ($0.0059) is within 1.5× of the rating’s. Time drift is nil: the same request two days later returns the same probabilities (r .995–.998 over 39,000 answers), and the sorts fitted from each day agree at .991+, so a cached answer is a permanent measurement. Wording is the perturbation that matters, and on a fact it does not: rewordings of “population” agree with each other as well as one wording agrees with itself across window draws. On soft attributes (which names read as VC-respectable, as having aura) rewordings agree at .5–.9, negation is ignored outright, a persona replaces the question, and no pooling of wordings closes the .6–.75 ceiling against Fable — the definition-carrying wording, repeated, is the recipe.

Setup

Cohorts. countries: 198 sovereign states from Wikidata with their latest population; the reference is log population, so Spearman measures accuracy against a fact and the fitted slope measures cardinal calibration. arxiv150: 150 arXiv abstracts scored on a one-paragraph definition of novelty; the reference is gemma-4-31b’s pooled z-score on the 40 that overlap the multi-criteria benchmark, so ρ is judge–judge and self-agreement is the precision statistic for all 150.

Recipes. A round shows every item once, in windows of k. rate: one ten-level standing question per item (“where does item x stand among those shown”), k questions a call. yes/no all pairs: “x is stronger than y” for every ordered pair, k(k−1). ratio all pairs: the polarity-safe nine-rung ladder (“far weaker: 1/8 or less” … “far stronger: 8 times or more”) for every ordered pair. ratio cycle: the same ladder on a random Hamiltonian cycle only, both orders, 2k. adaptive variants re-fit after each round and window consecutive blocks of the current order with a random offset. anchored wide ratio: three items pinned at the 10 / 50 / 90 % points of a one-round rating pilot (charged) sit in every window beside k−3 targets, each target against each anchor and the anchors against each other on a 1/100 … 100 ladder.

Fit and score. Pairs are least squares on the directed log-ratio (ladder expectation) or logit (yes/no); ratings are least squares with a window fixed effect, which is what makes sorted windows usable. After every round: Spearman and Pearson against the reference, split-half by round parity stepped up by Spearman–Brown (self), bits per item as ½ log₂ 1/(1−r²), and the dollars billed so far. The first round of adaptive recipes is random by construction.

1 · Agreement against dollars

On countries every recipe climbs to the same ceiling and the x position of the climb is the whole result: rating at k = 24 is at ρ .97 after 2 rounds and $0.0024, rating at k = 8 after 3 rounds, the all-pairs ratio after 2 rounds but $0.0193, because a round of 56 ladder questions on 8 items costs 7× a round of 8 ratings and carries no more about the order. Yes/no on all pairs is the cheap all-pairs form (a yes/no question is a third of a ladder question in tokens) and is the second-cheapest recipe overall. On abstracts the same ordering holds against a softer ceiling: everything sits at ρ .88–.91 against gemma from round 3 on, and the self-agreement panel is where the recipes separate — rating reaches .95 first, the anchored ladder reaches the highest final value.

So: the information per dollar is set by the number of questions a call needs to place its items, not by their form. A rating places k items with k questions; every pairwise design spends at least 2k and gets the same latent.

2 · Dollars to a threshold

Read as bits: at its first round the k = 24 rating carries 233k bits of population information per dollar (198 items × 1.4 bits per item for $0.0012), k = 8 rating 169k, yes/no all pairs 101k, the ratio cycle 69k, the all-pairs ratio 26k. The metric saturates by design — bits per item is bounded by what the judge knows, so past the ceiling every added dollar buys zero — which is why the threshold cost is the operational number.

3 · Cardinal calibration

The 1/8 … 8 ladder compresses to .40–.40 nat per nat whether asked on all pairs or a cycle, adaptive or not: a country ten times as populous is called “about 2 times” on average. The wide ladder against pinned anchors recovers .76 at k = 8 and .73 at k = 24, and it is the only recipe whose ratio answers can be read as magnitudes without a calibration step. Yes/no logits are on their own scale (.85 logit per nat) and are ordinal in practice.

So: for an ordinal answer any recipe is calibrated enough; for a cardinal one, pin anchors, widen the ladder to the range the truth spans, and still expect a quarter of the dynamic range to be missing.

4 · Adaptive windows

Sorting after each round and comparing neighbours is the classic way to spend comparisons where the order is uncertain. Here it bought little: adaptive rating reaches the threshold at the same round and price as random windows on both cohorts, and adaptive ratio cycles clear the countries threshold one round earlier ($0.0091 against $0.0121), still 2× the rating. Two reasons are visible in the data. The ceiling arrives in two or three random rounds, before an adaptive design has anything to exploit. And at k = 24 the adaptive rating’s self-agreement falls to .994 on countries and .998 on abstracts against .998 and .998 for random windows: windows of near-equal items give a rating question nothing to spread its levels over, and the window fixed effect then absorbs most of what it does say.

So: random windows, repeated, are the design; adaptive refinement is worth revisiting only for a cohort where the plateau takes more than five rounds.

5 · Time drift

Two days apart, a yes/no probability moves on average 0.012 and a nine-level expectation 0.09 of a level; the fitted sorts agree at .991 or better and agree with Fable to the same third decimal. That is bf16 batching wobble (the .03 already known), not drift. A cached answer can be reused indefinitely; there is no reason to re-ask a question for freshness.

6 · Can Jev tell when to hand off?

A system that uses Jev where it can and a stronger model where it cannot needs a signal for “cannot”. Jev does not carry one per item: the rank correlation between its own uncertainty and its error against Fable is -.04–.24, and escalating by it is the random curve. The information exists — an oracle handing over six of 24 items closes most of the gap to Fable — but Jev’s errors are systematic opinions it holds with the same confidence as its correct ones, which is the same fact as “more consistent than right” seen from the other side. Disagreement with a second cheap judge (gemma) is a slightly better signal (.14–.23) and beats random by a few hundredths on the prose cohorts. At the level of a whole attribute the picture is better: Jev–gemma agreement predicts Jev–Fable agreement at r 0.51 over the 36 cells.

So: decide Jev-or-frontier per attribute, from a small pilot against the strong judge, not per item from Jev’s confidence; a second cheap judge is worth more as a disagreement detector than as a second vote.

7 · Where the ceiling is: seven facts

Self-agreement is .98+ on every cohort; accuracy runs from .73 (company revenue) to .98 (country population), and the three recipes agree on the ordering of the cohorts. That is the knowledge-ceiling map the attribute-level switch of § 6 needs: the gap between what Jev repeats and what is true is a property of the fact, not of the recipe, and it is visible in one cheap round. Where the ceiling is low the anchored recipe earns its price early — on cities, mountains and films its first round ($0.0060) is where the rating gets after six — because the pinned anchors connect every window to an absolute reference from the start. The wide ladder’s slope is near 1 where the truth spans about the ladder’s range (mountains 1.25, rivers 1.15) and compresses where the truth spans more (companies .55): match the ladder to the range.

So: one rating round on 24-item windows tells you the domain’s ceiling for about a tenth of a cent; a ceiling under .85 is where a stronger model or a richer state (facts in the text, not a bare label) belongs.

8 · The same question asked eight ways

At temperature zero re-asking is not a test (§ 5), so the perturbation that matters is the wording. On a fact it barely matters: every positive rewording of “population” agrees with the canonical one at .98+ on the same windows and .88–.92 on fresh ones, which is the same-wording floor of .91: the variance is in which items share a call, not in the words. Eight wordings pooled reach the truth at .97, eight rounds of one wording at .97. On the soft attributes the words are the dominant source of variance. Rewordings of “VC respectability” agree with the canonical one at .06 (a partner skimming a deal list) to .76 (the formal register); “rounds raised” at .51–.71. A pool of eight wordings self-agrees at .821–.918 where eight rounds of one wording self-agree at .966+: the repeated number is the reliability of a sentence, the pooled number is the reliability of the concept, and on soft attributes they differ by a tenth.

Two wordings fail outright. Negation is not read. “How little aura does x have (highest = the least)” sorts as aura, raw ρ .88 with the positive question on the same windows; “how few people live in x” is half-reversed (.47); only “how straight does x sound” follows the flip (-.40, and that is a different noun, not a negation). Jev reads the attribute noun and the ladder, not the polarity of the sentence around it, so a polarity-flipped wording is a second attribute, never a check. A persona rewrites the question. “A partner at a top venture firm … where does x land for her” shares .06 with the definition; a casting director ranking by presence shares .43 with “aura”. The scene replaces the construct.

So: diverse wordings are a validity probe, not a way to buy accuracy — run three or four positive rewordings once and read their agreement as the reliability of the concept; then spend the rounds on the one wording that carries the definition. Never flip polarity, never stage a persona.

9 · Soft attributes of names: how far Jev is from a frontier judge

These are the high-dimensional questions with no fact behind them, where a name is sorted by what a reader projects onto it. Two Fable reads agree at .88–.95, so the perception is stable enough to be a reference. Jev under the canonical wording reaches .73 on aura and .67 on VC respectability, .59 on sounds-gay and .50 on rounds raised, and eight rounds lift those to .75, .77, .64, .63 while its self-agreement sits at .97: the § 6 pattern, a confident opinion that is only partly the frontier model’s. The wording that carries the definition is the one that tracks Fable — canonical and formal lead on every attribute; the short casual and plain forms lose a tenth to a third; pooling eight wordings does not close the gap (.70 against .77 on VC), because the gap is what Jev perceives, not how it is asked. Aura is the attribute Jev shares with Fable most, rounds raised least — a two-step inference (name → founder → funding history) that a 0.6B judge does not make.

So: soft social attributes of bare names sit at a ceiling of .6–.75 against a frontier judge, below the .85 line of § 7: this is the class that escalates whole, and where the pilot-against-Fable of § 6 pays for itself in one round.

10 · The whole judgment space on one grid

This is the whole space asked at once, on the same items, priced the same way: how many things a judgment looks at (one item rated among k; two items, in a two-item state or as a pair inside a window; three items; the whole window as a choice), what kind of answer it gives (a yes/no, a level out of 3, 5, 7 or 10, a ratio on a nine-rung, five-rung or 1/100…100 ladder, the one option that is highest), how many items share a state (2, 3, 4, 8, 24, 48) and how they are laid out (labelled blocks, plain numbered lines). The winner is rate-L10-k24: 24 items in a labelled state, one ten-level standing question per item, random windows, repeated. Composite ρ .837 at half a cent; and the largest amount any of the other 28 recipes beats it by, on any cohort at any budget, is .027 (rate-L3-k24 on films at $0.002), inside the .03 run-to-run wobble of the judge. Its runners-up are its own variants — 48-item windows (worst gap .030), five levels (.031), three levels at k = 8 (.035) — and the first recipe from another family, choice of highest and lowest at k = 8, has a worst gap of .037. Nothing below the rating family is ever the best by more than the wobble, and on the soft attribute every other family loses outright.

Level count is ordinally irrelevant and cardinally decisive. Three, five, seven and ten levels at k = 24 land within .01 of each other on every fact; the fitted slope against log population goes .25 → .43 → .73 → .95, so ten levels are free precision. Window size: k = 4 starts a tenth behind (ρ .87 on countries and .60 on names at $.002 against .97 and .71) because each call carries only three comparators; 8 is a hundredth behind 24 at the same spend; 48 buys nothing over 24. Format: plain numbered lines equal labelled blocks on facts and lose a tenth on bare names (.63 against .75) — a name needs a label saying it is a name.

Per-item yes/no (“x is in the top half of those shown”) is the cheapest question that works on facts — countries ρ .968 for $0.0042, at $0.0007 a round — and loses on names (.67): a binary answer discards the within-half order, which on a soft attribute is most of the signal. Choice of one collapses with k: “which is highest” at k = 8 tops out at .84 on countries with self-agreement .82, and at k = 24 falls to .58–.66, because one probability mass over 24 options is one fact per call however it is split; adding “which is lowest” rescues k = 8 (composite .822, at $0.0007 a round the best cheap recipe on facts) and not k = 24. Three at a time equals the rating when the triple sits inside a 24-item window (.817) and pays for the missing context when the state is only three items: tri3 reaches the same final ρ on facts but starts at .83 on countries and .56 on names at $.002, sixty-six calls a round each seeing nothing but its three.

Pairwise never wins, and on the soft attribute it is the wrong instrument. On facts the all-pairs ratio ladder reaches the highest final ρ of anything (.983 on countries, .913 on films) for $0.0579, eight times the rating’s six rounds, and by $.005 every pairwise form (yes/no all pairs, yes/no cycle, ratio cycles at nine, five and wide rungs) is where the rating already was at $.002. On names the ceiling itself drops: yes/no all pairs .61, the ratio cycle .59, the wide cycle .48, anchored .40, against the rating’s .75 — a question about x against y on a bare label draws on the thinnest thing Jev has, while a rating of 24 names in one state has the whole distribution in view and the anchors of an anchored ladder, pinned from a pilot on a soft attribute, pin noise. Two-item states are the worst use of a dollar in the grid (yes/no .70 on countries and .28 on names at $.002; the ratio form .83 and .29 at $.005): every call pays the instruction for one comparison.

Nor does what is done with the winner’s answers matter. Replaying its traces offline (refit.py) and reading the top level or a pooled-threshold probit latent instead of the PMF expectation, or fitting with PMF-variance weights, Huber IRLS, or slot fixed effects, moves ρ by at most .007 on any cohort (names with the top level +.026, with self-agreement down .02). The window has a real position effect — the first slot is rated up to half a level high (companies +.52 levels, se .05; the trend is −.002 to −.016 levels a slot) — and random windows absorb it: a slot fixed effect costs .03–.07 at one round, where each item has sat in one slot, and returns at most .002 after two.

So: the unambiguous best sorting system on Jev is the plainest one, and the grid closes the question rather than leaving it to taste — no pairwise, triple, choice or binary design beats a 24-item ten-level rating by more than the judge’s own wobble anywhere, and every one of them loses by .05–.35 somewhere. Spend the design effort on the state (labelled, definition-carrying, 24 items) and on the attribute-level escalation of § 6, not on the question form.

Recipes llmsort should endorse

  1. Ordinal sort, any size — the default: random windows of 24 items in labelled blocks, one ten-level standing question per item, window fixed effect in the fit, repeated. Composite ρ .837 at $.005 over the § 10 grid and never more than .027 behind anything; ρ ≥ .97 on 198 countries at $0.0024; $0.0121 to self-agreement .95 on 150 abstracts. Three levels sort as well as ten; ten are free cardinal precision.
  2. When a per-pair read is wanted (a specific comparison must be defensible, or the criterion may be unstated): yes/no on all ordered pairs in a window of 8, logit fit. Same ceiling on facts at 1.2× the price; a tenth worse on soft attributes, so never there.
  3. Cardinal answer: three pinned anchors from a one-round rating pilot, wide ladder spanning the true range, every target against every anchor. Highest self-agreement, slope .76 against truth.
  4. Weak-knowledge domains: anchored wide ratio, one round, ladder matched to the range of the truth — the best first-round number wherever the ceiling is under .9.
  5. Escalation: pilot each new attribute on ~24 items against a frontier judge; keep Jev where it agrees, hand the attribute (not individual items) to the stronger model where it does not.
  6. Wording: one wording that states the attribute definition, repeated on fresh windows; three or four positive rewordings once as a validity probe (their agreement is the reliability of the concept); no negated forms, no personas.
  7. Do not spend on: all-pairs ratio ladders for an ordinal answer (5× the price for the same rank information); two- or three-item states (the instruction is paid per comparison); choice-of-one questions over more than eight options; plain-line formats for bare labels; any pairwise form on a soft attribute; adaptive windows before the random plateau is measured; re-asking for freshness; pooling diverse wordings for accuracy.

Limits: one judge; one wording per question form except in § 8; the abstracts reference is a 31B generating judge on 40 items, so ρ there is bounded by its reliability and self-agreement carries the comparison.
